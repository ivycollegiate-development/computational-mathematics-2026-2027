## PART 1 — WHAT YOUR MODEL ASSUMES (15 min)

No code, no notes. Answer in full sentences.

1. Your pipeline is: raw series → line fit → day-of-week offsets → subtract →
   MAD scale → threshold. **Write, in one sentence each, what must be true for
   that to be a valid model.** ______
2. How many assumptions is that? ______
3. **Which single one is most likely to break on real data?** ______
4. What is the failure mode of each of these, in one line each?

| if this breaks… | the output looks like… |
|---|---|
| the trend is not linear | |
| the weekly cycle drifts | |
| noise is not independent | |
| the scale is not constant | |
| an event lasts many days | |

- ☐  5. Which of those five failures would your detector **catch**, and which
     would it **cause**? ______

That last question separates detectors from generators, and the honest answer is
that several of these *cause* problems rather than being caught by them. An event
lasting five days is not a five alerts; a sustained event drags the fitted trend
and the day-of-week offsets with it, so the detector normalises the anomaly away
over the following week. **A model that adapts fast enough to catch a brief
event will adapt fast enough to absorb a persistent one.** There is no setting
of that speed that avoids the trade — only a decision about which failure you
prefer, made explicitly.

## PART 2 — THE METALOGICAL QUESTIONS (12 min)

6. What is the difference between "the model fit the data well" and "the data
   were generated by the model"? ______
7. Your detector finds 2 of 2 planted events. **What does that prove about the
   real world?** ______
8. Your `sd/mad` ratio on event-free residuals is `0.947`. **What does a ratio
   near 1.0 tell you, and what does it not tell you?** ______
9. Name a situation where a model with a wrong specification gives a *more
   confident-looking* answer than a correctly specified one: ______
10. So: what is the difference between a model that is **precise** and one that
    is **valid**? ______

Questions 6, 7, and 10 are the same question approached three ways, and they are
the intellectual spine of the whole course. You planted two events. The detector
found two events. That is a statement about a program, on data you generated,
with the answer key in your pocket. The *method* generalises or it does not; the
*result* generalises to nothing at all. Precision is what you measured. Validity
is what you have not tested, and the only honest way to describe it is as an
assumption list — which is what Part 1 just made you write.

- ☐  11. So in your defense, the word "validated" may only be used if ______
- ☐  12. What word should you use instead, and in what sentence? ______

## PART 3 — THE DEFENSE PLAN (12 min)

Tomorrow. Five minutes, and you have to rehearse it today on paper.

- ☐  13. Write the **opening claim** — one sentence, the thing you built and
     what it does: ______
- ☐  14. Write the **numbers sentence** — threshold, caught, false rate, all in
     one breath: ______
- ☐  15. Write the **limits sentence** — the assumption most likely to break,
     stated plainly: ______
- ☐  16. Write the **what-I-would-need** sentence — the data you lack: ______
- ☐  17. What is the one question you expect, and what is your answer? ______
- ☐  18. Which of 13–16 is the one you are most tempted to skip? ______

The last one is the real question. Everyone writes 13 and 14, and 15 is where
the grade lives. A defense that reports only successes reads as a sales pitch,
and a skeptical listener's default assumption becomes that you are hiding
something — which costs you more credibility than the omission was worth. Say
the limit before you are asked for it, and the rest of the talk gets easier.

## 🇹🇼 TAIWAN CONTEXT

The Part 2 questions are not philosophy here; they are the difference between a
model that gets reviewed and one that gets trusted. Locally, a great deal of
operational reporting is generated by models fitted to a short historical window
and then carried forward, and the reporting treats the output as a measurement
when it is an extrapolation. The instinct to state the assumption list is
uncommon enough to be worth practising deliberately, and it is the single most
effective thing a person can add to a report that a model produced.

The Part 1.5 trade — fast adaptation versus persistent-event absorption — is also
real and familiar. A detector tuned to be responsive catches a burst and then
stops noticing, because the burst has become the new normal inside a week. A
detector tuned to be stable ignores bursts entirely. There is no free setting,
and the correct response is not a cleverer algorithm but an **explicit policy
about which of the two you are buying**, written down where the people who
inherit the dashboard will find it. Inherited dashboards that nobody wrote down
the reasoning for are how this failure becomes permanent.
