Question 8 is the one with the real answer, and it is probably not the one
people expect. Arithmetic errors — sign, arithmetic, transcription — decline
fairly reliably, because they are loudly wrong and immediately corrected.
**Reasoning errors do not decline nearly as much**, and they are the ones that
cost you on a paper test under time pressure. The tags that persist are the
judgment ones: misreading what a question asks, trusting a summary statistic,
declining to check an edge case, believing a plausible number.

## 🇹🇼 TAIWAN CONTEXT

The year-long arc of this course has a local dimension that is easy to state and
hard to overclaim, so it is worth stating carefully. Across eight units you
repeatedly built the same habit in different clothes: take a number someone else
produced, ask how it was computed, and reproduce it yourself. In a context where
reporting and dashboards are often taken at face value — locally, in operations
and in anything that arrives as a summary — that habit is the difference between
using a number and being used by one. The most valuable local practice is not
memorising a method; it is the reflex of **reproducing one figure by hand** and
seeing whether it matches.

There is a second thing worth naming without overclaiming, because it is the
honest lesson of the whole course. A large share of the failures we found —
including the ones I shipped in the first drafts of these lessons — were not
mathematical errors at all. They were **plausible numbers that nobody ran**. The
math was almost always right; the claim about the output was not. That is the
failure mode this course was built around, it is not a lesson about being
careless, and the specific skill that prevents it is small: run the thing, read
the actual output, and only then write it down. Nearly everything worth keeping
from this year is downstream of that.
