## PART 1 — THE THREE THINGS THIS UNIT WAS ACTUALLY ABOUT (12 min)

Unit 2 was three lessons wearing one costume. Name each one, in your notes, with
a concrete example from your own project:

1. **Visualization** — getting a picture to say one true thing clearly. Which of
   your three charts did this best? What made it clear rather than merely
   informative?
2. **Data integrity** — cleaning, auditing, and knowing what you do not have.
   Name one thing your data could **not** answer, no matter how good your chart was.
3. **Privacy and honesty** — the audience is real. Your data came from a campus
   network; someone was behind every row. What did you have to be careful *not* to
   show?

The point of naming all three: a visualization is only as good as the honesty of
the pipeline that produced it. Perfect rendering of a lie is still a lie.

## PART 2 — GALLERY: WHAT MAKES A SECURE VISUALIZATION? (25 min)

We will walk the room's projects as a class gallery — screens up, no presenting
required. For each project you see, you are looking for **one security question**:

> If I built this chart, who could be hurt by it, and what would I have to change?

Work through these together, out loud:

- The campus network dataset has daily `failed_logins` counts. A chart of that
  makes a bad week look worse than it was. What is the honest fix?
- A chart of phishing reports by class period could let someone guess **which class**
  fell for them. Same data, new risk. What changes?
- Your histogram showed days with unusually low login success rates. Naming those
  **dates** in your write-up is more useful to your security team — and more
  dangerous to whoever caused them. Where is the line?

There is no single right answer here. There is a defensible one, and you have to
be able to say why yours is defensible.

## PART 3 — THE PRINCIPLE (15 min)

Write, in your own words and in 3-4 sentences, your answer to this:

**What is the one rule you will follow for the rest of this class — and probably
the rest of your life — about making charts from data that is not yours?**

Rules you may not use as your answer: "be accurate" or "label everything." Those
are table stakes, not principles. I want the rule that only you would write,
because it came from something that actually went wrong in your own work.

Then: what is the **hardest** part of keeping that rule when you are in a hurry
and someone is waiting for a chart?
